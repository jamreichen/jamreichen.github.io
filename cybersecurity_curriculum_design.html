Proposal Overview

A portable course architecture built for approval, adoption, and real learning.

The proposal connects curriculum strategy, national standards anchors, safe lab design, authentic assessment, privacy, responsible AI use, and career awareness. Every module asks learners to produce evidence—not simply recall vocabulary.

Design thesis

Cybersecurity education should teach judgment before confidence.

The course begins with authorization, ethics, digital citizenship, and responsible disclosure. Technical depth grows through systems, networks, identity, cryptography, hardening, coding, data analysis, organizational defense, incident response, AI security, and leadership.

Practice remains bounded by explicit scope: isolated environments, resettable virtual machines, synthetic logs, fictional organizations, classroom-safe tools, and documented verification or recovery steps.

01

Ethical before technical

Authorization, acceptable use, responsible disclosure, privacy, and stakeholder communication are established before learners perform defensive technical work.

02

Evidence over intuition

Learners use logs, diagrams, source/context/content/channel evidence, tests, and reproducible observations to justify conclusions and recommendations.

03

Safe practice environments

Labs use synthetic data, simulators, isolated networks, prebuilt scenarios, fictional advisories, and resettable systems rather than live exploitation.

04

Human-validated AI

AI-supported work requires approved data, prompt hygiene, source checking, independent validation, attribution, uncertainty, and documented limitations.

Scope & Sequence

Five units build a complete foundations pathway.

Select any unit to reveal its module progression, evidence package, safe performance tasks, and standards emphasis. The unit grouping below follows the module ranges stated in the proposal headings.

Unit01
Modules 1–4

Cyber Foundations

Establish legal and ethical practice, model computing systems, trace network communication, and diagnose protocols, ports, services, and connectivity.

Authorization Careers Systems OSI / TCP-IP Troubleshooting

Module progression

  1. Cybersecurity, ethics, authorization, and careers
  2. Computing systems, abstraction, and decomposition
  3. Networking foundations and the OSI model
  4. Protocols, ports, services, and troubleshooting
Essential progression: define what work is permitted, understand the system, explain how data moves, then use approved evidence to isolate a fault.

Required evidence & safe performance

  • Ethics and authorization decision memo, signed lab compact, and inclusive NICE role profile
  • System decomposition and trust-boundary model
  • Annotated network diagram and packet-journey explanation
  • Troubleshooting log with commands, observations, conclusion, and verification

National anchors include digital citizenship, acceptable use, computing systems, communication, protocols, network design, and protection practices.

Unit02
Modules 5–8

Securing Digital Systems

Apply core security principles, reason through threats and human risk, design identity and access controls, and select cryptographic tools for secure communication.

CIA Triad Threat Modeling Social Engineering Identity & Access Cryptography

Module progression

  1. Security principles and threat modeling
  2. Threat actors, social engineering, and human risk
  3. Identity, authentication, and access control
  4. Cryptography and secure communication
Essential progression: define the risk, verify suspicious communication with evidence, constrain access, then match cryptographic controls to the security problem.

Required evidence & safe performance

  • Threat model and control-rationale matrix
  • Spoof Detective evidence log and security-awareness micro-lesson
  • Access-control audit and revised least-privilege permissions matrix
  • Cryptography concept model and scenario-based control recommendation

National anchors include confidentiality, integrity, availability, information protection, social-engineering awareness, authentication, access control, and cryptography.

Unit03
Modules 9–12

Secure Computing and Analysis

Harden endpoints, prioritize vulnerabilities, build and test defensive software, and transform synthetic logs into reproducible, appropriately qualified conclusions.

Endpoint Hardening Vulnerabilities Secure Coding Testing Log Analysis

Module progression

  1. Operating systems and endpoint hardening
  2. Software, patching, vulnerabilities, and disclosure
  3. Algorithms, secure coding, testing, and debugging
  4. Data representation, analysis, logs, and monitoring
Essential progression: establish a secure baseline, prioritize remediation, encode defensive reasoning in a tested artifact, then analyze event evidence reproducibly.

Required evidence & safe performance

  • Hardened endpoint baseline with before-and-after verification and rollback documentation
  • Vulnerability prioritization board and responsible patch/disclosure brief
  • Tested defensive program, source code, test table, and reflection
  • Log-analysis notebook or spreadsheet with reproducible steps, visualization, and uncertainty

National anchors include operating-system security, software security, algorithms and programming, network operations, data analysis, testing, and documented validation.

Unit04
Modules 13–16

Protecting Organizations and Data

Design layered defenses, assign responsibility across connected services and devices, protect sensitive information, and translate legal, ethical, and operational requirements into usable policy.

Defense in Depth Cloud / Mobile / IoT Privacy Data Protection Policy & Governance

Module progression

  1. Network attacks and layered defense
  2. Cloud, mobile, IoT, and shared responsibility
  3. Data protection, privacy, identity, and digital footprints
  4. Cyber law, intellectual property, policy, and governance
Essential progression: connect attack patterns to controls, assign responsibility, protect data throughout its lifecycle, and make requirements usable through policy.

Required evidence & safe performance

  • Layered-defense evaluation and revised network diagram with residual risk
  • AI Security Advisor device review and shared-responsibility matrix
  • Privacy and data-protection plan plus an AI Prompt Safety decision log
  • Policy/compliance brief and a testable security and AI-use policy

National anchors include network and computer security, cloud and IoT, personal information, data protection, law, intellectual property, policy, and governance.

Unit05
Modules 17–20

Cyber Operations and Leadership

Assess organizational risk, build resilience, respond to incidents without destroying evidence, evaluate AI-system security, and communicate a coherent capstone strategy.

Risk & Resilience Incident Response Forensics AI-System Security Capstone

Module progression

  1. Risk, resilience, backup, and recovery
  2. Incident response, digital forensics, and evidence
  3. AI literacy and AI-system security
  4. Capstone: protecting an organization
Essential progression: prepare for failure, preserve evidence under pressure, govern AI use and AI-system risk, then integrate the full course into a defensible organizational strategy.

Required evidence & safe performance

  • Risk register and verified backup/recovery test plan
  • Incident timeline, chain-of-custody form, response critique, and deepfake evidence brief
  • AI-cyber risk and validation memo, source-check log, and secure-use checklist
  • Capstone portfolio and technical/executive briefing based only on simulated or isolated systems and fictional data

National anchors include loss prevention, risk treatment, incident response, evidence, network operations, AI governance, communication, and NIST CSF 2.0 vocabulary as supplemental guidance.

Standards, Approval & Market Research

From one course map to a 51-jurisdiction decision system.

The companion research package is designed to help curriculum leaders evaluate standards alignment, approval pathways, pedagogy, implementation requirements, and market positioning across all fifty states and the District of Columbia. The cards below describe the intended analysis layers; they do not substitute for the full jurisdiction matrix or report.

The jurisdiction matrix can document current standards, framework versions, course-code or subject-area fit, graduation-credit implications, and the national anchors used to support a portable core.

The analysis can distinguish where approval is primarily state-led, locally controlled, CTE-mediated, or dependent on district curriculum review, teacher assignment rules, acceptable-use expectations, and cybersecurity lab safeguards.

The course design emphasizes constructive alignment: essential questions, three lesson experiences, observable demonstrations, required evidence, safe performance tasks, and standards anchors reinforce one another within every module.

The research report can translate the standards and approval landscape into practical product decisions: course positioning, implementation supports, teacher resources, platform requirements, lab constraints, accessibility, and phased launch priorities.

Behind the Prototype

Go behind the scenes of how Spoof Detective was created.

The playable simulation is only the final layer. The companion case study traces how the experience moved from a cybersecurity learning objective to a structured investigation loop—then through research, wireframes, interaction design, accessible development, testing, and iteration.

Explore the design process →
01

Design the learning experience first

The prototype begins with the learner, the performance objective, and the evidence needed to demonstrate sound phishing judgment. The core loop asks learners to inspect, identify, explain, decide, and reflect rather than simply guess whether a message is safe.

Learner analysis Objectives Scenario design Evidence of mastery
02

Translate the strategy into an investigation interface

Wireframes and interaction patterns were shaped around the reasoning task: keep the simulated message readable, make evidence inspectable, and give learners a clear place to record clues and make a defensible decision without overwhelming the screen.

Wireframes Interaction design Visual system Accessibility
03

Build, test, and refine the system

The final prototype uses reusable interface components, synthetic phishing scenarios, feedback tied to learner reasoning, and classroom-safe interactions. Testing focuses on whether learners can notice meaningful evidence, explain risk, and transfer that judgment to new scenarios.

Reusable components Safe simulation Feedback logic Validation
