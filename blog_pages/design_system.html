My interactive learning experience design system

How I design lessons learners can move through.

This is the seven-phase process I have developed through curriculum leadership, software engineering, classroom instruction, and hands-on prototyping. I use it to turn learning goals into clear, engaging, accessible, and reusable HTML experiences.

An experience-informed design approach

I treat visual design and learning architecture as one connected system.

I created this process by combining what I have learned from curriculum development, technical program leadership, software engineering, game and interaction design, teaching, and observing real learners use digital experiences. It preserves the speed and creativity of visual exploration while adding the instructional structure, technical planning, and testing needed to make the final experience work in practice.

I developed this approach as a practical working system—not a rigid linear methodology. I move backward and forward whenever a prototype, learner response, technical constraint, or visual test reveals a better direction.

I design the learning before I decorate the screen.

Before I invest in complex artwork, I establish the experience format, repeated learner action, assessment logic, and feedback model. That gives every later visual decision a purpose.

01

Phase one

Define the learning experience

I begin by anchoring the project in a measurable learning goal, then choose an interaction model that lets the learner demonstrate it.

1

Establish the learning goal

I define what students should know or be able to do by the end of the experience, along with the conditions in which they must demonstrate it.

  • Target learner and grade level
  • Learning objective
  • Academic standards
  • Expected session length
  • Prior knowledge
  • Evidence of mastery
  • Required accessibility level
  • Device and browser requirements
Example objective

Students will identify three indicators of a phishing email and correctly decide how to respond.

2

Select the experience format

I choose the interaction model that best reveals the target knowledge or skill. I do not add a game format merely to make the lesson feel more entertaining; the format must make the learning more visible, authentic, or memorable.

Guided simulation
Branching scenario
Interactive investigation
Decision-making challenge
Puzzle or escape room
Digital lab
Role-playing experience
Card-sorting activity
Narrative adventure
Build-and-test challenge
3

Define the core experience loop

I identify what the learner repeatedly does. This loop creates the rhythm of the experience, limits unnecessary interaction patterns, and makes the learner’s thinking visible enough to assess.

Observe→Investigate→Decide→Receive feedback→Advance

For phishing: Open message → Inspect clues → Mark evidence → Choose response → See consequences.

02

Phase two

Plan the content and flow

I map the learner journey before styling it, inventory the real content, and define exactly how feedback and mastery will work.

4

Create the experience map

I map every screen and meaningful path before I design the visuals. This helps me identify missing transitions, dead ends, overloaded screens, and decisions that do not actually change the experience.

  • Entry or mission briefing
  • Instructions
  • Primary challenges
  • Feedback moments
  • Optional clues or help
  • Incorrect-answer recovery
  • Assessment
  • Completion screen
  • Replay or next-step options
  • Branch points and reconnection points
5

Create a content inventory

I list everything the experience needs before interface decisions become dependent on placeholder content. Realistic content often changes the amount of space, hierarchy, pacing, and interaction support the design requires.

  • Instructions and dialogue
  • Questions and answers
  • Images and characters
  • Emails or documents
  • Evidence and clues
  • Sound effects
  • Feedback messages
  • Scores, badges, and progress
6

Define assessment and feedback

I define how the experience will measure learning and how each action will help learners improve. Feedback is part of the instruction, not a decorative message added after the interaction.

  • Actions that earn points
  • Retry rules
  • Hint penalties or support
  • Mastery threshold
  • Action-level feedback
  • Final results communication
Feedback standard

Explain why an action was correct or incorrect. Do not stop at “Correct” or “Try again.”

03

Phase three

Establish the visual direction

I use rapid reference gathering, deliberate visual distillation, representative mockups, and a restrained design system to define realism, tone, hierarchy, and interaction states before complex production begins.

7

Collect references

I gather references in clear categories: interface layouts, real-world tools, characters, environments, color, typography, icons, animation, sound, and atmosphere. At this point I prioritize range and useful evidence over presentation quality.

For realistic simulations

Collect references from the actual tools learners would encounter—such as email clients, security dashboards, or messaging systems.

8

Build the mood board

I organize references in Milanote, PureRef, or Figma, but I do not treat the first board as a final answer. It is a fast visual hypothesis that helps me identify emotional tone, realism, age appropriateness, visual density, interface style, character style, lighting, and motion.

“Sophisticated enough for high school students. Realistic interfaces with stylized narrative elements. One important decision per screen.”

My fast visual-direction loop

I start loose, then make the visual direction progressively more specific.

The goal is not to make the first mood board beautiful. The goal is to externalize possibilities quickly, discover what the experience actually needs, and create enough evidence to make better visual decisions.

01

Slap together a mood board

I collect anything that captures a useful quality: a believable email layout, an interesting lighting treatment, a character silhouette, a color relationship, a transition, or a sense of atmosphere. I allow contradictions at this stage because contrast helps me see the available directions.

Output: a broad, imperfect reference field.
02

Narrow to the must-haves

Using the learning goal and experience requirements as the filter, I identify three to five visual qualities the design cannot lose. These might include realistic source material, mature character styling, low visual clutter, evidence that feels discoverable, or a clear single-screen focal point.

Output: a short list of visual non-negotiables.
03

Extract a working color scheme

I sample relationships from the strongest references, then convert them into functional roles: page background, surfaces, primary action, selected evidence, warning, success, danger, and subdued information. I adjust the palette for contrast and readability rather than copying reference colors literally.

Output: a role-based, accessibility-aware palette.
04

Write the shorter visual vision

I compress the board into a one-page direction that another designer or developer could use without seeing every reference. It describes the intended feeling, realism level, density, visual hierarchy, interaction distinction, motion behavior, and what the design should avoid.

Output: a concise visual vision document.
05

Mock up representative art

I apply the direction to one demanding, representative screen—not the easiest screen. I document what supports the learning, what competes for attention, what feels too young or too corporate, what would be expensive to reproduce, and what breaks when realistic content is inserted.

Output: a test screen plus a decision log.
06

Feed the result back into the process

I use the mockup as new reference material. I revise the mood board, must-haves, palette, and visual vision, then test again. Each cycle should remove ambiguity and make the system easier to apply across the complete experience.

Output: a clearer direction with fewer unresolved choices.

My one-page visual vision

What I capture after the mood board

  • Experience statement: one sentence describing what the learner should feel and do.
  • Must-have traits: three to five qualities that define the direction.
  • Avoid list: visual signals that would make the experience feel childish, artificial, cluttered, or misleading.
  • Realism balance: which interfaces must be authentic and where stylization can support story or motivation.
  • Palette roles: colors assigned to actions, information, status, and assessment—not merely swatches.
  • Hierarchy rule: how the learner recognizes the current goal, available action, evidence, and feedback.
  • Motion rule: when animation explains state or direction and when it should remain absent.
  • Production constraints: reusable art, responsive behavior, asset limits, and browser performance.
What I document after each mockup

Evidence, not vague preference

Keep: What immediately supports tone, clarity, or authenticity?

Change: What competes with the task or obscures interaction?

Question: What still needs learner, stakeholder, or technical validation?

Systemize: Which successful decisions can become reusable components or rules?

9

Establish the design system

Once the direction survives a representative mockup, I translate it into a restrained set of reusable rules and states. I design the components before I produce an entire library of polished screens.

  • Primary, secondary, and accent colors
  • Success, warning, danger, and neutral colors
  • Heading and body fonts
  • Type and spacing scales
  • Button, card, and panel styles
  • Icon style
  • Focus, hover, selected, and disabled states
  • Animation speed and behavior
04

Phase four

Design the interface

I solve hierarchy, navigation, pacing, and clarity in grayscale before I invest in polished visuals.

10

Sketch and wireframe

I create rough layouts for information hierarchy, primary actions, navigation, dialogue, evidence, progress, feedback, and accessibility controls.

Wireframe test

A screen should remain understandable without color or polished artwork.

11

Build a low-fidelity prototype

I use realistic text quantities and clickable blocks to test spacing, reading order, click count, menus, navigation, movement, decision flow, feedback timing, and smaller-screen behavior.

12

Conduct a clarity test

I ask representative users to complete the experience without verbal guidance. I observe behavior before asking for opinions because hesitation, missed information, and incorrect clicks often reveal more than preference feedback.

  • Where do they look first?
  • What appears clickable?
  • Do they understand the goal?
  • Where do they hesitate?
  • What information do they miss?
  • Does feedback improve the next attempt?
  • Can they recover from mistakes?
05

Phase five

Create the visual experience

I add final art, motion, and atmosphere while protecting clarity, readability, responsiveness, and the distinction between decoration, information, and interaction.

13

Produce the high-fidelity design

I add final typography, color, lighting, illustrations, characters, realistic interface details, environmental art, icons, transitions, sound cues, and microinteractions only after the experience structure is stable.

  • Decorative elements
  • Interactive elements
  • Informational elements
  • Assessment elements
14

Apply composition and hierarchy rules

Visual hierarchy: I place the primary objective, clue, or action where the learner is most likely to look first, using size, contrast, spacing, and motion sparingly.

Aspect ratio and responsiveness: I choose a base viewport, then define minimum width, scaling, panel wrapping, hotspot alignment, mobile behavior, and maximum text width.

Composition: I use lighting, contrast, gaze direction, environmental shapes, and leading lines to guide attention.

Readability: I define minimum font size, line length, line spacing, color contrast, button size, text-over-image rules, captions, and instruction standards.

06

Phase six

Build and validate

I create reusable components, separate content from interface logic, and validate learning, usability, accessibility, and technical reliability.

15

Create the HTML prototype

I build reusable components rather than treating every lesson as a separate application. Whenever practical, I keep lesson content separate from interface code so the same experience engine can support multiple lessons.

Mission briefing
Dialogue
Email or document viewer
Evidence inspector
Multiple-choice decision
Drag-and-drop activity
Hotspot scene
Hint system
Feedback panel
Progress tracker
Results screen
JSON lesson content
16

Test the complete experience

I test four areas, then verify supporting details such as loading time, focus, scoring, retries, saved state, audio controls, and accidental refreshes.

AreaCore question
LearningDoes the experience actually demonstrate mastery of the objective?
UsabilityCan students understand what to do without teacher intervention?
AccessibilityCan it be completed with keyboard navigation, screen readers, captions, and sufficient contrast?
TechnicalDoes it work across required screens and browsers without losing progress?
17

Pilot and improve

I pilot with a small group of representative students and diagnose whether problems come from content, interface, instructions, or difficulty—not simply whether students liked it.

  • Completion rate
  • Time per challenge
  • Incorrect-answer patterns
  • Hint usage
  • Abandonment points
  • Assessment results
  • Student confidence
  • Student comments
07

Phase seven

Package the work for reuse

I preserve the decisions, assets, logic, and evidence needed to scale the process across lessons, teams, and designers.

18

Document the completed experience

I save the complete design record so the experience can be maintained, evaluated, handed off, and adapted without recreating the reasoning from scratch.

  • Learning brief
  • Experience map
  • Script and content
  • Mood board
  • Design system
  • Component inventory
  • Assessment logic
  • Accessibility checklist
  • Testing results
  • Final source files

The compact system

The compact version of how I work.

Define→Select→Map→Assess→Reference→Distill→Mock up→Wireframe→Prototype→Test→Style→Build→Validate→Improve

The decision filter

I use the learning triangle as my final decision filter.

The strongest lesson designs maintain a firm connection between what students must learn, what they repeatedly do, and what proves they have mastered it. When a visual or interaction does not strengthen that connection, I simplify it, revise it, or remove it.

01

Learning objective

The knowledge or skill the experience is designed to develop.

02

Learner action

The repeated behavior that makes thinking visible and keeps the experience active.

03

Evidence of mastery

The observable performance that proves the objective was achieved.
