The central idea

I did not want to build a quiz about phishing. I wanted to build an investigation in which the learner's thinking becomes visible.

The game is organized around a simple design triangle: students must identify meaningful phishing evidence, act on that evidence, and explain or demonstrate a safe response. Story, scoring, badges, interface realism, and visual polish only earn a place when they reinforce that relationship.

JR
My project approachEvidence-informed · learner-centered · technically reusable

I treated the process as iterative rather than linear. Learner assumptions shaped the wireframes; competitor research shaped the interaction model; visual tests changed the hierarchy; and technical constraints changed how the evidence inspector and feedback system were structured.

I use artifacts to move the project forward: a brief, empathy map, comparison matrix, experience map, visual vision, wireframes, component plan, and validation log. The artifacts make decisions visible and prevent style from outrunning purpose.

01

Phase one · Define and empathize

I began with the learner's decision—not the game's aesthetic.

I framed the learning objective, documented assumptions about high-school learners, and used an empathy map to identify where confidence, urgency, jargon, and fear of being wrong could affect performance.

Explore phaseClose phase

The first design question was not “What should the screen look like?” It was “What should a learner be able to notice, decide, and explain after the experience?” That kept the project centered on observable cybersecurity behavior.

1

Set the learning brief

I defined a practical design target for a short, web-based experience that could fit into a high-school cybersecurity, digital citizenship, or introductory computing lesson.

  • Target learner: high-school students with basic email familiarity
  • Session target: approximately 25–35 minutes
  • Primary objective: identify meaningful phishing indicators and choose a safe response
  • Prior knowledge: email fields, links, attachments, basic account security
  • Mastery target: correctly justify safe action in at least 80% of scenarios
  • Devices: laptop or tablet, keyboard and touch compatible
  • Accessibility: non-color cues, visible focus, reduced motion, readable evidence
  • Safety: no live malicious links, downloads, or credential entry
Learning objective

Students will identify multiple indicators of a phishing attempt, explain why those indicators matter, and select an appropriate response such as reporting the message, verifying through a trusted channel, or avoiding the link or attachment.

2

Build the empathy map

I used the empathy map as an evidence-informed hypothesis about how a high-school learner might approach the challenge. The map focuses on behavior and emotional friction, not demographics or stereotypes. Each cell below is clickable.

Says

I treated statements of confidence as a hypothesis to test. A learner may recognize obvious spelling errors yet miss sender-domain mismatches, hover-state URLs, or the safest reporting action.

Learner-lens statement

For a high-school student who uses email but has limited incident-response experience, the game should make subtle evidence visible without making the answer obvious, so the learner can practice slowing down, verifying, and choosing a safe next action.

3

Connect objective, action, and mastery

The learning triangle

Objective + learner action + evidence of mastery

The objective is not “finish five levels.” The repeated learner action is inspect, mark, decide, and explain. Mastery is visible when the learner consistently finds meaningful cues and chooses a safe response without relying on a superficial “looks fake” judgment.

02

Phase two · Research and compare

I researched both phishing content and the learning patterns used by relevant games.

I reviewed official phishing guidance, human-detection research, browser-based safety games, phishing quizzes, and enterprise gamification to identify useful patterns and gaps for a high-school audience.

Explore phaseClose phase

The comparison was not about copying a competitor. It was about understanding what each format makes easy to learn—and what it leaves out.

4

Research the content model

I used official guidance to validate the clues, responses, and debrief language. I also used the NIST Phish Scale concept as a reminder that detection difficulty depends on both message cues and the learner's context—not simply the number of obvious mistakes.

  • Unexpected or mismatched sender identity
  • Urgent or threatening language
  • Suspicious links or domains
  • Unexpected attachments or downloads
  • Requests for credentials or sensitive information
  • Verification through a trusted, independent channel
  • Report, delete, and protect the account
  • Scenario difficulty that increases gradually

Jigsaw Phishing Quiz

Realistic recognition practice
Useful pattern
A quick browser-based phishing self-check keeps attention on realistic message cues and immediate explanation.
Gap for my goal
A linear quiz does not fully support a role, evidence collection, incident timeline, or branching response.
My response
I kept realistic messages and clue-specific feedback, then added an investigative role, marked evidence, consequences, and a multi-step incident.
Source

Google Interland

Playful progression and feedback
Useful pattern
Clear worlds, score, progress, replay, and recognizable learning themes make safety content approachable.
Gap for my goal
Reality River addresses fake content broadly, but the visual language and action model are younger and less like a real inbox.
My response
I retained progression, feedback, and confidence-building, but used a mature high-school tone and an authentic email-and-dashboard interaction.
Source
Official Interland page

FBI Safe Online Surfing

Historical classroom benchmark
Useful pattern
The former program used grade-based activities, testing, teacher participation, and school competition to support classroom adoption.
Current status
The FBI states that the SOS Challenge ended June 1, 2026, and teacher signups, student testing, and the national competition were discontinued.
My response
I kept the idea of a classroom-ready sequence, but designed a self-contained experience that does not depend on account registration or competition.
Source
Official FBI program notice

Enterprise phishing games

Behavior practice and rewards
Useful pattern
Enterprise game libraries use realistic scenarios, split-second decisions, points, badges, and performance feedback to reinforce behavior.
Gap for my goal
The context is generally workplace-focused, proprietary, and aimed at adult employees rather than students learning the reasoning for the first time.
My response
I adapted the behavioral practice and rewards, reduced corporate language, added explicit teaching, and used a school-relevant narrative.

Define the opportunity

Opportunity statement

Create a high-school experience that feels more authentic than a cartoon safety game, more active than a quiz, and more explanatory than a workplace simulation—while remaining short, accessible, and reusable in a classroom.

High-school context

The interface needed to feel mature enough for secondary students while remaining clear for learners with uneven cybersecurity vocabulary and different levels of email experience.

8

Define the five-mission experience flow

Mission 1

Investigate

Open the suspicious email and mark sender, link, urgency, and attachment evidence.

Mission 2

Analyze

Compare the message with account activity, alerts, and trusted information.

Mission 3

Timeline

Reconstruct what happened and separate evidence from assumptions.

Mission 4

Respond

Choose how to report, contain, verify, protect the account, or preserve evidence.

Mission 5

Debrief

Review consequences, explain decisions, replay, and transfer the skill.

9

Design assessment and feedback

I separated mastery evidence from motivational scoring. A student could earn fewer bonus points after using a hint while still demonstrating mastery on a later attempt.

Evidence marking

What did the learner notice?

Selected sender, URL, urgency, attachment, and request clues are recorded separately from the final classification.

Reasoning

Why is it suspicious?

Feedback connects the clue to risk instead of displaying only “correct” or “incorrect.”

Response

What should happen next?

The learner chooses a safe action: verify independently, report, delete, protect the account, or preserve evidence.

Transfer

Can the learner apply it again?

Later scenarios reduce obvious cues and change context so the learner cannot rely on memorizing one pattern.

Feedback model

“Good catch. The display name says IT Support, but the domain does not match the school's official address. Verify the request through a known school channel instead of replying or clicking.”

04

Phase four · Direct the visual system

I used the supplied images as private references—not as page decoration or reusable assets.

I extracted general qualities such as friendliness, human presence, technology metaphors, and palette relationships, then created an original visual direction for the game and this case-study page.

Explore phaseClose phase

The goal of reference analysis is to understand why an image works, not to transplant the image into a new page.

!
Reference-only policy

The supplied stock-style illustrations are not embedded, reproduced, traced, or used as decorative website assets. I analyzed them only for broad visual qualities: friendly vector characters, inclusive human presence, light blue fields, blue-purple technology shapes, yellow action accents, and clear metaphors such as a code folder, workstation, question, growth arrow, and target.

10

Translate references into design principles

What I retained

Approachable human-centered tone

Characters and familiar learning/work contexts reduce intimidation and make cybersecurity feel learnable rather than mysterious.

What I retained

Clear symbolic metaphors

Folders, shields, magnifiers, targets, and progress markers communicate purpose quickly without requiring dense explanation.

What I changed

A more mature high-school interface

I shifted from generic corporate illustration toward a realistic inbox and security dashboard with restrained character support.

What I rejected

Exact artwork and composition

I did not reuse character poses, layouts, image crops, or decorative scenes. The final system uses original CSS, SVG, icons, and interface components.

11

Distill the visual vision

One-page visual vision

Friendly enough to invite exploration. Realistic enough to teach transfer.

The experience should feel vigilant, empowering, playful, and trusted. The inbox and dashboard carry the realism; original vector icons, badges, and restrained character moments provide warmth and motivation. One meaningful decision remains visually dominant on each screen.

12

Use AI for exploration—with human review and rights guardrails

I used AI tools as assistants for research organization, idea generation, style exploration, scenario variants, and code scaffolding. I did not treat generated text or imagery as authoritative or automatically publishable.

Research support

Organize sources and questions

AI helped build search terms, summarize candidate sources, compare game patterns, and identify questions that required official-source verification.

Idea generation

Expand scenarios and mechanics

I generated alternate phishing contexts, distractors, mission names, badge ideas, feedback drafts, and consequence patterns, then selected and rewrote the strongest options.

Style exploration

Test visual directions quickly

Prompt-based visual exploration helped compare dark cyber-game, bright educational, realistic inbox, and character-guided directions before I distilled original rules.

Prototype support

Generate variants, not decisions

AI assisted with wireframe alternatives, microcopy, component scaffolding, and QA test ideas. Final hierarchy, pedagogy, accessibility, and code behavior remained human decisions.

  • Verify phishing guidance against official sources.
  • Do not use real malicious links, files, or credential collection.
  • Review scenarios for bias, stereotypes, and unnecessary fear.
  • Keep student data and personally identifiable information out of prompts.
  • Check image ownership, licensing, and originality before publication.
  • Label untested ideas as hypotheses rather than findings.

14

Run a clarity test

I would ask representative learners to complete the prototype without verbal coaching and observe behavior before asking for opinions.

  • Where do they look first?
  • What do they think is clickable?
  • Do they understand the mission?
  • Do they inspect the full sender address?
  • Can they distinguish evidence from decoration?
  • Do they know what to do after identifying a phish?
  • Can they recover after a wrong selection?
  • Does feedback improve the next attempt?

Phase six · Build and validate

I designed a reusable game engine rather than a single fixed lesson.

The technical plan separates content from interface components, supports keyboard and touch, records evidence and decisions, and makes it possible to add new scenarios without rebuilding the entire application.

Explore phaseClose phase

Reusable architecture matters instructionally: it makes scenario variety, progressive difficulty, and consistent feedback easier to maintain.

16

Design accessibility into the interaction

  • Keyboard access to every clue and decision
  • Visible focus and logical reading order
  • Text labels for status and icons
  • Non-color indicators for safe, suspicious, selected, and incorrect
  • Minimum readable type and comfortable line length
  • Large touch targets and no precision-only hotspots
  • Reduced-motion option and no required timed animation
  • Screen-reader announcements for clue status and feedback

07

Phase seven · Improve and reuse

I planned the pilot to reveal whether problems came from content, interface, instructions, or difficulty.

The final step preserves the reasoning, metrics, source notes, component rules, accessibility requirements, and scenario structure so later versions can improve without losing coherence.

Explore phaseClose phase

I would not judge the game only by whether students say they liked it. I would look for evidence that the experience changed what they notice and how they respond.

18

Pilot with representative learners

  • Completion rate and time per mission
  • First clue selected
  • Missed clue patterns
  • Incorrect response patterns
  • Hint use and retry behavior
  • Abandonment points
  • Confidence before and after
  • Performance on a new transfer scenario
19

Use a decision log

Keep

What supports learning and authenticity?

For example, the side-by-side email and evidence tray if learners use both successfully.

Change

What competes with the task?

For example, decorative badges shown during investigation if they distract from evidence.

Question

What still needs validation?

For example, whether a time pressure mechanic helps transfer or only increases guessing.

Systemize

What can become a reusable rule?

For example, every clue needs a label, a risk explanation, and a safe-action connection.
