# Learning Experience Design Approach

## Overview

Jamie Reichenberger approaches learning experience design as the intentional creation of systems that help people build knowledge, practice skills, receive meaningful feedback, and apply what they have learned in realistic contexts.

Her approach combines:

- Instructional design
- Learning science
- Curriculum strategy
- User experience design
- Software engineering
- Project-based learning
- Assessment design
- Educational technology
- Artificial intelligence
- Continuous improvement

Jamie does not view a course as a collection of lessons. She views it as a complete learner journey.

That journey includes the learner’s goals, prior knowledge, motivation, activities, tools, feedback, support systems, assessments, and opportunities to apply learning in authentic situations.

# Core Design Philosophy

## Design for the Learner’s Experience

Jamie begins with the experience the learner needs to have, not with the content that needs to be delivered.

She asks questions such as:

- Who are the learners?
- What are they trying to accomplish?
- What do they already know?
- What barriers could prevent them from succeeding?
- What should they be able to do at the end?
- How will they practice?
- How will they know whether they are improving?
- Where will they need instructor or peer support?
- What will make the learning feel relevant?

This learner-centered perspective helps prevent courses from becoming content-heavy but experience-light.

## Begin With Performance

Jamie designs backward from the real-world performance expected of the learner.

Instead of asking, “What information should be included?” she asks:

- What should learners be able to produce?
- What decisions should they be able to make?
- What problems should they be able to solve?
- What tools should they be able to use?
- What quality standards should they be able to meet?

The learning experience is then built around those performances.

## Make Learning Active

Jamie prioritizes active learning over passive consumption.

Learners should have frequent opportunities to:

- Practice
- Build
- Explain
- Analyze
- Create
- Test
- Reflect
- Revise
- Collaborate
- Solve realistic problems

Videos, readings, and demonstrations support learning, but they should lead into meaningful action.

## Connect Learning to Real Work

Jamie designs learning experiences that resemble the work learners will eventually perform.

Examples include:

- Building a functional application
- Conducting a usability study
- Debugging a software problem
- Designing a game level
- Analyzing a dataset
- Creating a technical presentation
- Writing professional documentation
- Responding to a realistic workplace scenario
- Presenting a project to stakeholders

Authentic work helps learners understand why the material matters and gives them evidence of what they can do.

# Learning Experience Design Framework

Jamie’s learning experience design process generally includes the following phases:

1. Discovery
2. Learner and context analysis
3. Outcome definition
4. Experience architecture
5. Activity and assessment design
6. Content development
7. Technology and delivery planning
8. Quality assurance
9. Implementation
10. Evaluation and continuous improvement

# Phase 1: Discovery

## Purpose

The discovery phase establishes why the learning experience is needed and what success should look like.

## Discovery Questions

Jamie may investigate:

- What problem is the organization trying to solve?
- Who requested the learning experience?
- What business or academic goal does it support?
- What do learners currently struggle with?
- What should change after the learning experience?
- What constraints must be considered?
- What existing materials or systems already exist?
- What does success look like for learners and stakeholders?

## Discovery Activities

Examples include:

- Stakeholder interviews
- Learner interviews
- Instructor interviews
- Existing-course reviews
- Competitor analysis
- Skills-gap analysis
- Workflow observation
- Performance-data review
- Support-ticket review
- Survey analysis

## Discovery Deliverables

Possible deliverables include:

- Project brief
- Learner profile
- Problem statement
- Success criteria
- Risk analysis
- Stakeholder map
- Scope definition
- Initial learning strategy

# Phase 2: Learner and Context Analysis

## Understanding the Learner

Jamie creates a practical learner profile based on evidence rather than assumptions.

The profile may include:

- Prior knowledge
- Professional goals
- Motivation
- Technical confidence
- Learning preferences
- Time constraints
- Accessibility needs
- Language considerations
- Device and internet access
- Common misconceptions
- Emotional barriers
- Support needs

## Understanding the Environment

The learning environment may affect design decisions.

Jamie considers:

- Online, in-person, hybrid, or self-paced delivery
- Instructor availability
- Cohort size
- Technology access
- Learning management system limitations
- Time available for completion
- Institutional policies
- Accreditation requirements
- Workplace expectations
- Required software or equipment

## Example Learner Profile

A learner profile for an online software engineering program might include:

- Adult career changers
- Limited weekly study time
- Mixed levels of technical experience
- Strong motivation but low technical confidence
- Need for clear milestones
- Need for frequent feedback
- Desire for portfolio-ready projects
- Need for career relevance

This profile would influence pacing, support resources, project structure, and feedback frequency.

# Phase 3: Outcome Definition

## Clear and Measurable Outcomes

Jamie defines learning outcomes that describe observable learner performance.

Strong outcomes use verbs such as:

- Build
- Analyze
- Design
- Implement
- Evaluate
- Debug
- Compare
- Test
- Present
- Document
- Justify

Weak outcomes such as “understand” or “learn about” are replaced with measurable actions.

## Example Outcome Transformation

Weak outcome:

> Understand usability testing.

Improved outcome:

> Plan and conduct a moderated usability test, analyze participant feedback, and recommend prioritized design improvements.

## Outcome Levels

Jamie may define outcomes at multiple levels:

- Program outcomes
- Course outcomes
- Module outcomes
- Lesson objectives
- Project milestones
- Assessment criteria

Each level should support the next.

# Phase 4: Experience Architecture

## Mapping the Learner Journey

Jamie maps how learners move through the experience from initial orientation to independent application.

A common progression includes:

1. Connect to prior knowledge
2. Introduce the purpose
3. Demonstrate the skill
4. Guide initial practice
5. Provide feedback
6. Increase complexity
7. Apply the skill independently
8. Reflect on performance
9. Transfer the skill to a new context

## Experience Flow

A well-designed learning sequence may include:

- A relevant opening problem
- A concise explanation
- A worked example
- Guided practice
- A short knowledge check
- An authentic challenge
- Feedback
- Revision
- Reflection
- Transfer activity

## Cognitive Load

Jamie reduces unnecessary cognitive load by:

- Breaking complex tasks into stages
- Using consistent structure
- Providing examples
- Clarifying instructions
- Removing irrelevant content
- Sequencing prerequisites
- Limiting simultaneous new concepts
- Offering optional support resources
- Using visuals where they improve understanding

# Project-Based Learning

## Why Jamie Uses Projects

Projects allow learners to integrate multiple skills and produce evidence of competence.

A strong project should:

- Align with learning outcomes
- Resemble authentic work
- Have clear requirements
- Allow meaningful decisions
- Include milestones
- Provide feedback opportunities
- Include quality criteria
- Produce a useful artifact

## Example Project Structure

A software project may include:

1. Problem definition
2. Requirements
3. Technical planning
4. Initial prototype
5. Instructor or peer feedback
6. Iteration
7. Testing
8. Documentation
9. Final demonstration
10. Reflection

## Portfolio Value

Jamie considers whether a project can help learners demonstrate their abilities to employers or stakeholders.

Portfolio-ready projects often include:

- A polished final artifact
- Documentation
- Reflection
- Screenshots or demonstrations
- Explanation of technical decisions
- Evidence of iteration
- Clear connection to real-world skills

# Assessment Design

## Assessment as Evidence

Jamie treats assessment as evidence of learning, not simply as a grading activity.

The assessment should show whether the learner can perform the intended outcome.

## Types of Assessment

### Diagnostic Assessment

Used before instruction to identify prior knowledge or skill gaps.

Examples:

- Pre-assessments
- Skills inventories
- Prior-knowledge prompts
- Sample tasks
- Confidence ratings

### Formative Assessment

Used during learning to guide improvement.

Examples:

- Knowledge checks
- Draft reviews
- Practice exercises
- Instructor feedback
- Peer feedback
- Reflection
- Milestone submissions

### Summative Assessment

Used to evaluate final performance.

Examples:

- Final projects
- Presentations
- Exams
- Demonstrations
- Portfolios
- Capstones

## Authentic Assessment

Jamie prefers assessments that resemble real performance whenever possible.

For example:

- A future developer builds and debugs an application.
- A game designer creates and playtests a level.
- An HCI student conducts a usability study.
- A data analyst interprets and communicates findings.
- An instructional designer develops a learning solution.

# Rubric Design

## Purpose of a Rubric

A rubric should make expectations visible before the learner begins.

Jamie designs rubrics that:

- Align with the learning outcomes
- Describe quality clearly
- Separate major skill areas
- Use observable criteria
- Support consistent grading
- Help learners self-assess
- Provide meaningful feedback

## Example Rubric Categories

For a software project:

- Functional requirements
- Code quality
- Technical architecture
- Testing
- Documentation
- User experience
- Presentation
- Reflection

## Performance Levels

Performance levels should describe meaningful differences in quality.

Example:

### Exemplary

The solution is complete, technically sound, well-documented, and demonstrates thoughtful decisions beyond the minimum requirements.

### Proficient

The solution meets the core requirements and demonstrates appropriate technical understanding with minor issues.

### Developing

The solution partially meets the requirements but contains significant gaps, errors, or unclear decisions.

### Beginning

The solution does not yet demonstrate the required skill or is incomplete.

# Feedback Strategy

## Feedback Should Be Actionable

Jamie designs feedback to help learners improve, not simply explain why they received a score.

Strong feedback identifies:

- What worked
- What needs improvement
- Why it matters
- What the learner should do next
- Which resource may help

## Feedback Timing

Feedback is most useful when learners still have an opportunity to revise.

Jamie may build feedback into:

- Project milestones
- Draft submissions
- Practice activities
- Peer reviews
- Office hours
- Automated checks
- Instructor comments
- Resubmission opportunities

## Example Feedback

Less useful:

> This section is unclear.

More useful:

> Your explanation identifies the feature, but it does not explain how the feature supports the user’s goal. Add one sentence connecting the design decision to the user research findings.

# Scaffolding

## Supporting Learners Without Removing Challenge

Scaffolding helps learners complete work they could not yet perform independently.

Examples include:

- Worked examples
- Templates
- Checklists
- Starter code
- Guided questions
- Partial solutions
- Visual models
- Decision frameworks
- Sample submissions
- Milestone deadlines

Support should gradually decrease as learners gain confidence and skill.

## Example Scaffolding Progression

### Early Activity

Learners follow a detailed tutorial.

### Intermediate Activity

Learners complete a partially structured task with prompts.

### Advanced Activity

Learners independently design and justify a solution.

# Accessibility and Inclusive Design

## Designing for More Learners

Jamie considers accessibility from the beginning rather than treating it as a final compliance step.

Design considerations may include:

- Clear heading structure
- Descriptive links
- Alternative text
- Captions and transcripts
- Keyboard accessibility
- Color contrast
- Plain language
- Multiple ways to engage
- Multiple ways to demonstrate learning
- Flexible pacing where possible
- Reduced unnecessary cognitive load

## Inclusive Examples and Scenarios

Jamie also considers whether examples, visuals, names, roles, and scenarios reflect a broad range of learners and professional contexts.

Inclusive design helps learners feel that the experience was designed with them in mind.

# Motivation and Engagement

## Relevance Before Decoration

Jamie does not equate engagement with visual effects or entertainment.

A learning experience is engaging when learners understand:

- Why the skill matters
- How it connects to their goals
- What progress they are making
- What they can do with the skill
- How the challenge is achievable

## Motivation Strategies

Jamie may use:

- Clear goals
- Meaningful choices
- Visible progress
- Authentic projects
- Short-term milestones
- Timely feedback
- Peer collaboration
- Reflection
- Real-world examples
- Portfolio outcomes

Jamie uses AI to accelerate parts of the design process while maintaining human responsibility for learning decisions.

AI may support:

- Learner research synthesis
- Outcome drafting
- Activity ideation
- Scenario generation
- Rubric drafting
- Content variation
- Feedback templates
- Accessibility checks
- Quality assurance

- Instructor resources

## Human Review

Jamie reviews AI-assisted work for:

- Accuracy
- Bias
- Relevance
- Learning alignment
- Accessibility
- Tone
- Technical correctness
- Academic integrity
- Appropriate difficulty

## Example AI-Assisted Workflow

A designer may provide:

- Learner profile

- Delivery format

- Activity options
- Scenario drafts
- Rubric criteria
- Feedback prompts

# Instructor Experience

## Designing for the Instructor

Jamie believes a learning experience must work for instructors as well as learners.

Instructor-facing design may include:

- Teaching guides
- Facilitation notes
- Common misconceptions
- Grading examples
- Feedback guidance
- Technical troubleshooting
- Discussion prompts
- Pacing recommendations
- Escalation procedures
- Sample student work

## Why Instructor Support Matters

Even a strong course can produce inconsistent results if instructors do not have clear support.

Jamie designs systems that help instructors:

- Understand the purpose of each activity
- Identify learner difficulties
- Grade consistently
- Provide useful feedback
- Adapt without losing alignment
- Troubleshoot technical problems

# Quality Assurance

## Reviewing the Entire Experience

Jamie evaluates more than grammar and formatting.

Her quality assurance process may review:

- Outcome alignment
- Technical accuracy
- Assessment validity
- Instruction clarity
- Workload
- Accessibility
- Consistency
- Tool functionality
- Learner flow
- Instructor usability
- Content duplication
- Prerequisite gaps

## Example Quality Assurance Checklist

Before launch, Jamie may verify that:

- Every activity supports an outcome
- Every outcome is assessed
- Instructions are complete
- Required tools are accessible
- Projects have clear success criteria
- Rubrics match the assignment
- Examples are technically correct
- Support resources are available
- Accessibility needs are addressed
- Instructor guidance is complete

# Evaluation and Continuous Improvement

## Learning Experiences Are Never Truly Finished

Jamie uses evidence to improve courses after launch.

Possible data sources include:

- Completion rates
- Assessment performance
- Learner feedback
- Instructor feedback
- Support requests
- Time-on-task data
- Revision patterns
- Course analytics
- Portfolio quality
- Employment outcomes

## Evaluation Questions

Jamie may ask:

- Where are learners stopping?
- Which concepts create the most confusion?
- Which assignments require the most clarification?
- Are assessments measuring the intended skills?
- Are learners receiving feedback early enough?
- Are instructors grading consistently?
- Are projects producing strong portfolio artifacts?
- Are the tools helping or creating friction?

# Example Case Study 1: Redesigning a Technical Course

## Challenge

Learners were completing lessons but struggling to apply concepts independently in the final project.

## Design Response

Example actions:

- Replaced several passive lessons with guided practice
- Added project milestones
- Added a worked example
- Introduced draft feedback
- Revised the rubric
- Added troubleshooting resources
- Reduced duplicated content

## Example Metrics to Replace

- Final-project completion increased from 68% to 84%
- Average project score increased by 14%
- Assignment clarification requests decreased by 30%
- Learner confidence increased by 22%
- Course completion increased by 11%

# Example Case Study 2: Improving Instructor Consistency

## Challenge

Multiple instructors interpreted assignments and rubrics differently.

## Design Response

Example actions:

- Created an instructor guide
- Added annotated sample submissions
- Clarified rubric language
- Added grading calibration sessions
- Documented common misconceptions
- Created feedback templates

## Example Metrics to Replace

- Grading variance decreased by 35%
- Instructor onboarding time decreased by 40%
- Rubric-related questions decreased by 50%
- Learner grade appeals decreased by 18%
- Instructor satisfaction increased to 92%

# Example Case Study 3: Designing a Portfolio-Based Program

## Challenge

Learners completed coursework but graduated without enough polished evidence of their skills.

## Design Response

Example actions:

- Added portfolio artifacts to major courses
- Designed milestone-based projects
- Added presentation requirements
- Added documentation standards
- Added peer critique
- Connected projects to job-role competencies

## Example Metrics to Replace

- 90% of graduates completed at least four portfolio artifacts
- Employer interview invitations increased by 20%
- Portfolio completion increased from 55% to 88%
- Career-coach satisfaction increased by 25%
- Graduate confidence increased by 19%

# Example Case Study 4: Reducing Learner Friction

## Challenge

Learners were losing time because of unclear instructions and inconsistent technical setup.

## Design Response

Example actions:

- Simplified setup instructions
- Added screenshots and short demonstrations
- Created a troubleshooting guide
- Added a pre-course technology check
- Reorganized resources
- Standardized assignment instructions

## Example Metrics to Replace

- Technical support tickets decreased by 38%
- Time to complete setup decreased from 90 minutes to 35 minutes
- First-week withdrawal decreased by 12%
- Assignment submission errors decreased by 27%
- Learner satisfaction increased by 16%


# Summary

Jamie’s learning experience design approach is practical, systems-oriented, learner-centered, and grounded in measurable performance.

She designs experiences that help learners:

- Understand why the learning matters
- Practice meaningful skills
- Receive useful feedback
- Build confidence
- Produce evidence of competence
- Transfer learning into real-world settings

Her goal is not simply to create content.

Her goal is to create learning experiences that work.
